# Hook Strategy

A strong static ad is not just a nice-looking image with a product name on it. The click comes
from a **belief disruption** or **high-signal payoff** that feels worth resolving right now.

Use this document when the current hooks feel safe, generic, or too "SaaS-y."

## Creative stack

Think in four layers:

1. **Angle** — the reason this ad exists (`pain_agitation`, `product_proof`, `comparison`, etc.).
2. **Creative mechanic** — how the viewer arrives at the truth (`reframe`, `contrast_without_comment`, `implied_answer`, `overheard_conversation`, `social_witness`).
3. **Hook tactic** — the specific scroll-stopping frame (`contrarian`, `warning`, `confession`, `curiosity`, `shocking_statement`, `price_anchor`, `offer_only`, etc.).
4. **Visual format** — the composition that expresses the idea (`ui_screenshot`, `before_after_split`, `comparison_table`, `native_social_post`, `offer_first_banner`, etc.).

Angle answers **why this message**.
Mechanic answers **how the meaning lands**.
Hook tactic answers **how we win the stop**.
Visual format answers **what the ad looks like**.

## What recent research suggests

- Recent Motion creative research frames hooks as durable tactics, not one-off trends: hook tactics are the strategic frame, while psychological triggers are the emotional mechanism inside that frame.
- Motion's 2026 benchmark materials cluster high-performing hook tactics into two useful families:
  - **Concrete / promotional:** newness, sale announcement, price anchor, urgency.
  - **Pattern-interrupt / cognitive:** confession, contrarian, shocking statement, warning.
- For skeptical or sophisticated buyers, contrarian, warning, confession, and proof-backed reframes recur more often than upbeat brand-slogan copy.
- For static Meta work, recent benchmarked workhorse formats include offer-first banners, demos, testimonials, before/after, headline-led statics, listicles, and us-vs-them comparisons.

This does **not** mean every ad should become clickbait. It means each spec should choose a
clear hook tactic and back it with proof.

## Provocative, but earned

Provocative hooks work when they attack one of these:

- a bad default behavior
- a category myth
- an expensive old way
- a hidden cost
- a familiar frustration stated more sharply than competitors say it

Provocative hooks fail when they do this instead:

- make a dramatic claim with no proof
- sound like a generic marketing slogan
- try to be edgy without naming a real tension
- imply outcomes the product cannot actually support

Rule: **break a belief, then pay it off with evidence.**

## Turn the volume up

If the first-pass concept feels tasteful, balanced, or merely "smart," it is usually too quiet
for cold paid-social traffic.

For the first wave of concepts, deliberately produce at least:

- one **medium** boldness concept
- one **hard** boldness concept

The goal is not to be reckless. The goal is to make the ad feel like it has an opinion.

High-boldness statics usually do one or more of these:

- attack an old way directly
- call out a mistake the viewer is still making
- name the hidden cost bluntly
- force a side-by-side contrast
- say the quiet part out loud in category language
- make the after-state feel dramatically better, cleaner, faster, calmer, or more impressive

Low-boldness statics usually sound like:

- "a better way to..."
- "streamline your..."
- "collaborate more easily..."
- "powerful platform for..."

That language explains. It does not interrupt.

## Boldness ladder

Use `strategy.boldness` intentionally:

- **soft:** credible, useful, non-polarizing; safer for warm audiences or regulated categories.
- **medium:** clear opinion, stronger claim, sharper contrast; usually a good default.
- **hard:** built to interrupt; more provocative, more blunt, more emotionally charged.

Hard boldness should still stay inside reality. It does **not** mean lying, promising impossible
outcomes, or manufacturing shock for its own sake.

## High-voltage tactics

If a concept needs more punch, start with these before rewriting everything else:

- **contrarian:** "The usual advice is the problem."
- **warning:** "Stop doing this before it costs you more."
- **shocking_statement:** "This workflow is killing your momentum."
- **comparison:** "Old way vs. the after-state."
- **price_anchor / cost callout:** "You are paying for the old way in time, stress, or money."

These tend to create more immediate attention than listicle or explanatory tactics.

## Boldness without slop

To make a static ad hit harder:

- shorten the hook until it sounds like something a blunt friend would say
- replace explanations with accusations, warnings, or contrasts
- give the old way a villain role
- make the visual contradiction visible from thumbnail size
- let one line dominate the frame instead of spreading the message across many smaller elements

Avoid:

- stacking three different provocative ideas in one ad
- using vague intensity words with no target ("crushing," "insane," "epic")
- shocking language that could apply to any category
- sounding aggressive without revealing what is actually wrong

## Outcome-first default

For most cold static ads, the viewer does **not** care about your interface, feature list, or
workflow taxonomy yet. They care about the **after-state**:

- the result they want
- the pain they want to escape
- the identity they want to become
- the friction they want removed

Default rule:

- **Sell the transformation first.**
- **Use the product, UI, or mechanism as support.**

This means a stronger first concept is often:

- a transformation
- a before/after
- an old-way/new-way contrast
- a lifestyle outcome
- a proof-backed emotional payoff

Instead of:

- an app screenshot
- a feature walkthrough
- a "here's what it does" explainer
- a generic platform claim

## When UI is useful vs. when it is not

UI or feature-led statics are usually best when one of these is true:

- the audience is already solution-aware or product-aware
- the interface itself is the proof of the claim
- the product has an unusually visual "wow moment"
- the goal is proof, objection handling, or lower-funnel conversion

UI or feature-led statics are usually a weak default when:

- the audience is cold
- the ad is top-of-funnel
- the claim is really about relief, status, confidence, speed, clarity, or identity
- the visual can be understood only if someone already cares about the workflow

Quick test: **If you blurred the interface, would the ad still sell the desired outcome?**
If not, the concept is probably too feature-dependent.

## Hook-writing workflow

For each creative, answer these in order:

1. **What belief or behavior are we interrupting?**
   Examples: "budgeting apps fix overspending", "manual review is good enough", "the expensive option must be better".
2. **Which hook tactic earns the stop best?**
   Use one primary tactic, not three blended together.
3. **What concrete proof makes the hook fair?**
   Demo, screenshot, comparison, sourced review, real stat, real offer, reply-to-comment, price math.
4. **What is the shortest human way to say it?**
   Prefer spoken language, not category jargon.
5. **What visual format reinforces the same idea?**
   The image should make the hook easier to believe, not merely decorate it.

## Tactics worth prioritizing for statics

### Contrarian
- **Use when:** the audience is educated, skeptical, or tired of category clichés.
- **What it does:** breaks a familiar rule or conventional advice.
- **Good pattern:** "X isn't the answer." / "The usual advice is why you're still stuck."
- **Needs:** a real mechanism or proof immediately after the interruption.

### Warning
- **Use when:** the buyer is actively shopping and could make the wrong choice.
- **What it does:** sincerely tells the viewer to stop before a mistake.
- **Good pattern:** "Don't buy another X until..." / "Before you choose Y..."
- **Needs:** a specific risk, not vague fear.

### Confession
- **Use when:** the category feels polished, corporate, or low-trust.
- **What it does:** disarms with vulnerability or candor.
- **Good pattern:** "I thought this was overkill until..." / "We got this wrong."
- **Needs:** believable humility and a concrete lesson.

### Shocking Statement
- **Use when:** the feed is saturated and you need a strong pattern interrupt.
- **What it does:** opens with a provocative claim that challenges assumptions.
- **Good pattern:** "Your workflow is wasting more time than meetings." / "This 'best practice' is the bottleneck."
- **Needs:** proof fast, or it reads as cheap clickbait.

### Curiosity
- **Use when:** the audience is not yet invested and needs an open loop.
- **What it does:** withholds just enough to create a curiosity gap.
- **Good pattern:** "The reason your paper review process keeps breaking..." / "Why smart teams still miss this..."
- **Needs:** a clean payoff; do not tease without resolving.

### Statistic / Price Anchor / Offer Only
- **Use when:** you have a real number, defensible savings math, or a real offer.
- **What it does:** makes the click feel economically rational.
- **Good pattern:** "$8,000 tool vs. one shared workspace." / "Free trial ends Friday."
- **Needs:** truthful numbers and a format that surfaces them instantly.

## Static-specific execution rules

- The **hook** can be a sentence, but the **overlay** should usually be shorter.
- For static ads, prefer **2-8 words** in the baked-in overlay and **1 dominant idea**.
- If the design looks too curated or balanced, it may disappear into the feed. Make the main line bigger, shorter, and more obvious.
- Three supporting callouts usually beat six tiny benefits on mobile.
- The most clickable static is often not the prettiest one. It is the one with the clearest information hierarchy and strongest interruption.
- When boldness is `hard`, the overlay should usually read like a statement, warning, or contrast, not a product descriptor.

## Mapping into the spec

Use these fields inside `strategy`:

- `angle` — the macro reason this ad exists.
- `boldness` — how forcefully the concept should interrupt (`soft`, `medium`, `hard`).
- `disruption_target` — the belief, behavior, old way, or cost this ad attacks.
- `hook_tactic` — the scroll-stopping frame.
- `psychological_trigger` — the emotional mechanism inside the hook (for example `pattern_interrupt`, `curiosity_gap`, `myth_busting`, `pain_agitation`, `social_proof_credibility`, `urgency_stakes`).
- `creative_mechanic` — how the viewer arrives at the truth (for example `reframe`, `contrast_without_comment`, `implied_answer`, `social_witness`, `overheard_conversation`, `trojan_horse`).
- `hook` — the actual line.
- `claim_support` — the proof that makes the hook fair.

If you cannot fill `claim_support` honestly, the hook is probably too aggressive for the proof you have.

## Good static hook patterns

- **Bad default:** "Manual review is the slowest way to collaborate on LaTeX."
- **Category reframe:** "The bottleneck isn't LaTeX. It's review chaos."
- **Warning:** "Don't send another paper draft as a PDF."
- **Confession:** "We built this because Git plus email comments was unbearable."
- **Curiosity:** "Why do research teams still lose edits in 2026?"
- **Comparison:** "Overleaf clone? No. Review workflow replacement."

## Outcome vs. feature examples

- **Feature-led weak:** "Collaborative LaTeX workspace with inline comments"
- **Outcome-led stronger:** "Turn review chaos into submit-ready drafts"

- **Feature-led weak:** "AI meal-planning app with grocery sync"
- **Outcome-led stronger:** "Eat like you have your life together"

- **Feature-led weak:** "Workout tracker with personalized routines"
- **Outcome-led stronger:** "Feel strong again in 20 minutes a day"

- **Feature-led weak:** "Dashboard for recurring subscriptions"
- **Outcome-led stronger:** "Stop leaking money every month"

## Weak hook patterns

- "The all-in-one platform for modern teams"
- "Seamless collaboration for everyone"
- "A better way to work together"
- "Powerful features for researchers"
- "Everything you need in one place"

These are category descriptions, not reasons to stop and click.

## Hook QA

Before approving a spec, ask:

1. Would a skeptical buyer actually pause for this?
2. What belief, risk, or desire is being interrupted?
3. What proof pays off the interruption?
4. Does the overlay read in under two seconds on a phone?
5. Could a competitor swap in their brand name and say the same thing?

If the answer to #5 is yes, the hook is still generic.
